Sectio I — The History

The Weight Is the Point

~100 Pounds of Rough Timber

The condemned did not carry the whole cross. He carried the patibulum — the crossbeam — lashed across his shoulders through the streets, to a stipes already standing at the place of execution. A hundred pounds of green, rough-hewn wood. That is the weight your congregation will feel.

Patibulum ferat per urbem.
0 Screws · Bolts · Brackets

Rome had no hardware store. The upright and crossbeam met in a mortise-and-tenon joint, driven tight with wooden dowels hammered by hand. Our cross is joined the same way — because when the beam settles into the upright with a sound you feel in your chest, no lecture is necessary.

1st Century Accuracy, Full Scale

Every dimension is drawn from the historical and archaeological record — the height of the stipes, the span of the patibulum, the forged square-shank nails. This is not a prop. It is a faithful reconstruction, and it changes the room the moment it enters.

The Patibulum

Crossbeam · ~100 lbs · Carried

The heart of the experience. Rough-hewn from solid timber, adzed by hand, with a full tenon cut into its center. This is the beam your people will shoulder — down the aisle, around the block, up the hill.

The Stipes

Upright · Mortised · Standing

The vertical post, mortised near the top to receive the patibulum's tenon. Sized true to the historical record — high enough to sober, low enough to see. It waits, upright, the way the Romans left theirs waiting.

Hand-Hammered Dowels

Wooden Pins · No Metal Fasteners

Oak dowels, shaved by hand, driven through the joint to lock beam to post. Assembly is part of the liturgy: the sound of the mallet carries to the back pew.

Forged Iron Nails

Hand-Forged · Square Shank · 7 in.

Three nails, hand-forged from iron stock to the pattern found at Givʿat ha-Mivtar — the only crucifixion nail ever recovered. Heavy in the palm. Passed hand to hand, they end conversations.

The Titulus

Replica Sign · Three Languages

The charge-board nailed above the condemned, lettered in Hebrew, Latin, and Greek: Iesus Nazarenus Rex Iudaeorum. Painted by hand on rough board, ready to mount.

Sectio III — The Craft

Built by Hands, for Hands

Mortise, cut by chisel — no two alike

We start with heavy, rough-sawn timber and take it backward in time — adze and drawknife, not sander and router. The surface keeps its tool marks because the original kept its tool marks. Roman execution stakes were not furniture.

The mortise is chopped by chisel and mallet. The tenon is cut to a snug, honest fit and pinned with oak dowels. The nails come off a forge, hammered square, one at a time. The titulus is lettered by hand.

Nothing about this product is efficient, and that is deliberate. You cannot mass-produce gravity. Every kit is built by one set of hands, checked against the historical record, and loaded onto the pallet like it matters — because where it's going, it does.

Per manus, ad manus. — By hands, into hands.
